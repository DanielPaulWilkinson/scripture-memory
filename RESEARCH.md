# What helps people remember scripture

The "paper research brief" could not be found on the box, in agent stores, or in The Scholar's and The Steward's recent work. These findings come from peer-reviewed memory research instead. Each technique below is one the app uses.

## 1. Retrieval practice (testing yourself)
Recalling a passage strengthens memory far more than re-reading it. Students who practised recalling a prose passage remembered much more a week later than those who re-read it four times.
- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249-255.
- Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. *Science*, 319(5865), 966-968.

**In the app:** every stage after the first asks you to produce the words, and the final stage gives only the reference.

## 2. Spaced repetition
Reviews spread out over growing gaps beat the same amount of cramming. The best gap grows with how long you want to keep the memory.
- Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 132(3), 354-380.
- Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological Science*, 19(11), 1095-1102.

**In the app:** each verse sits in a box with a review gap of 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 30 or 60 days. "Got it" moves it up a box, "Nearly" keeps it, "Not yet" sends it back to tomorrow. The home screen shows only what is due today.

## 3. Fading cues (first letters, then gaps)
Removing prompts bit by bit, so the learner supplies more each time, helps people learn long verbal material. First-letter cues are a strong aid for recalling text in order.
- Glisky, E. L., Schacter, D. L., & Tulving, E. (1986). Learning and retention of computer-related vocabulary in memory-impaired patients: Method of vanishing cues. *Journal of Clinical and Experimental Neuropsychology*, 8(3), 292-312.
- Riley, E., & Wilson, B. A. (2001). Errorless learning and the method of vanishing cues: A comparison of their effectiveness. *Neuropsychological Rehabilitation*, 11(1), 103-112.

**In the app:** full text, then first letters only, then progressively more words blanked, then the reference alone.

## 4. Saying it aloud (the production effect)
Words you say aloud are remembered better than words read silently.
- MacLeod, C. M., Gopie, N., Hourihan, K. L., Neary, K. R., & Ozubko, J. D. (2010). The production effect: Delineation of a phenomenon. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 36(3), 671-685.

**In the app:** each stage prompts "Say it out loud", then you reveal and check.

## 5. Chunking
Memory holds a few meaningful chunks at once, so long passages are easier learned in phrase-sized pieces.
- Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87-114.

**In the app:** verses are shown broken at their natural punctuation, one phrase per line.

## 6. Honest self-grading
People judge their own learning poorly straight after study; judging after a delay, from the cue alone, is far more accurate.
- Nelson, T. O., & Dunlosky, J. (1991). When people's judgments of learning (JOLs) are extremely accurate at predicting subsequent recall: The "delayed-JOL effect". *Psychological Science*, 2(4), 267-270.

**In the app:** you only grade yourself after the reference-only stage, by comparing what you said with the revealed text.
